The Mercury Economy

How thirty-six tiles of farms, mills and merchants feed, clothe and move their people, with no state to steer them, and what happens when moving goods gets cheaper.

Mercury is an open-source economy engine, calibrated on the nineteenth century, with a strategy game of nations, armies and treaties built on top of it. This page looks at the economy alone: no taxes, no tariffs, no roads built by a state, no new inventions. What is left is land, people, firms, markets and merchants, on the classic map of thirty-six hexagonal tiles.

Every number and chart below comes from running that map in the engine itself (the source is on GitHub). Click a tile on the map to see what its people do for a living once the map has settled.

Nothing in this economy is solved for. Four kinds of agent each want something simple, and act on it every day at the prices in front of them. The prices, the wages, the trade between tiles and where people live all come out of what they do.

The world: terrain, and what each tile does

Each hexagon is a tile, with its own people, firms and market. Tiles on the edge or next to the sea are coastal: they can fish, and ships carry goods past them cheaply.

I.Firms make things

Every tile has a set of firms: farms, mines and forests that only work where the land allows, and mills, smithies, tailors and breweries that can work anywhere. A firm hires labour from the tile's people, buys its inputs in the tile's market, and sells what it makes there. Its output follows a Cobb–Douglas production function:

output = TFP × levels1−s × laboura × input₁b × input₂c …, with a + b + c + … = s < 1

Levels are the firm's buildings, its capital. Owners add a level when the firm earns more than a level costs, and the exponents add up to less than one. So each extra worker in a given building adds less than the one before. shows this diminishing return for a single level, holding the other inputs at one unit each.

A firm can make its good in several ways, called methods. The first is the one available in 1820; later ones come with technology. In the full game nations research those technologies; here the methods are shown only to compare them. A later method is more productive (higher TFP) and often needs fewer hands (a lower labour exponent), but needs machinery or fuel the old way didn't.

One level of a firm: output as workers are added

How much does a firm make? Each day it looks at the prices in its tile, today's price for its good, the wage, and the prices of its inputs, and plans to make what pays best at those prices. It hires workers and buys inputs until the last worker or the last ton of coal adds exactly as much to its sales as it costs. Its target is the textbook competitive one: it makes up to the point where the price equals its marginal cost. It does this because it takes the price as given, as one of many small producers would. A tile's "brewery" stands for all its brewers, and no one of them can move the price of beer by brewing more or less.

A monopoly plans differently. As the only maker of its good, it knows that selling more lowers the price, so a unit more earns it only its marginal revenue: the price, less what the lower price loses on all the rest. It makes up to the point where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. That is less than a competitive industry would make, and it charges more. With demand of elasticity ε, the monopoly's last unit earns the price times (1 − 1/ε), so it sets a markup over marginal cost. No firm in Mercury has market power unless it is given it; the experiment below gives it to one. One tile's brewery becomes a monopoly, facing demand of elasticity 2 (beer substitutes for tea at that elasticity). Its rivals are the merchants who bring beer from the neighbouring tiles, as far as carriage allows: the more of the tile's beer they bring, the smaller the monopoly's share, and the less its own sales move the price. repeats this at each cost of transport.

A monopoly, and the free trade that erodes it

When carriage is dear, nobody can bring beer in, and the monopoly is worth about twice what the competitive brewery earns: it brews half as much and sells it dearer. As carriage gets cheaper, merchants bring beer from the neighbours whenever the monopoly's price is worth the trip, and the markup shrinks. "Free trade is the best antitrust policy" is an old saying, and here it shows. Once trade is nearly free, the monopoly earns less than the competitive brewery did. It still holds back its brewing, taking the merchants' shipments as given, as Cournot's firms do. But the merchants simply bring in more, and holding back only hands them its trade.

II.Markets set prices; merchants move goods

Each tile has a market for every good. The price rises when buyers want more than sellers offer and falls when there is too much. Nobody sets prices; they move a little each day toward the level where supply and demand meet.

The market

is
not an agent but a place: one for every good in every tile, and one for labour
each day
takes everyone's orders at today's price. If buyers want more than sellers offer, each buyer gets the same share of what was offered, and the other way round. Then it moves the price by the excess: up when buyers wanted more, down when sellers did, by a tenth of the gap a day at most

Between neighbouring tiles run merchants. A merchant buys a good where it is cheap and ships it to a neighbour where it is dear, if the price gap pays for the journey. Shipping uses up transport, a good of its own: carters, horses, boats and later railways, made by transport firms from labour. Each unit carried needs transport in proportion to the distance and the good's bulk, and perishables lose part of themselves on the way. Merchants trade only with neighbours; a good crosses the map by being sold on from tile to tile.

The merchants

are
each tile's trading house, with one purse and a route for every good to every neighbour
want
to buy cheap and sell dear: a route earns the price where it sells, on what arrives, less the price where it buys and the transport it needs
know
the prices at both ends of each route, and what is still unsold at the far end
decide
how much to buy on each route: a little more each day while the margin is positive, a little less when it isn't, and less again when goods are left unsold at the far end. When the purse is short, the routes with the best margins are funded first

The price of a good, and where it flows

Colour is the price, averaged over the last 30 days of a run at normal transport costs. Arrows are the merchants' shipments, thicker for more. Goods flow downhill in price, from where they are made to where they are dear.

The merchants' trade pins prices to each other. Call a good's landed cost on a route the price it is bought for at the origin plus the transport a unit needs, allowing for what spoils on the way. If the destination pays more than that, the route makes money and the merchants ship more. Extra supply then pushes the destination's price down, and extra buying pushes the origin's up, until the gap is gone. If the destination pays less, nobody ships. So once markets settle, a good's price in a tile that imports it equals its neighbour's price plus the cost of carriage, and no tile's price can stay above that for long.

Prices settle at the neighbour's price plus carriage

Carriage is paid per unit carried, in proportion to the distance and the good's bulk, not to its value. Carrying a chest of tea costs as much as a chest of potatoes, but the tea is worth far more, so the same carriage is a small share of its price. Alchian and Allen pointed out the consequence in 1964: per-unit transport costs make goods worth much for their bulk travel further than cheap, bulky ones. Hummels and Skiba confirmed it in trade data forty years later, under the title "Shipping the good apples out".

tests it, with spoilage switched off so that only carriage decides how far a good goes. III.What transport is worth

Transport cost is one number in the game that scales every hop. To see what it does, the same map was run for fifteen years at each of 25 costs, and the last year measured: from nearly free (a twentieth of the normal cost) to 128 times normal, and finally to autarky, where no route ever pays and every tile lives on what it makes itself.

As transport gets dearer every measure of well-being falls smoothly (). Living standards drop because each tile must make more of its own goods, including the ones its land is bad at, and people pay more for imports. Output per head falls even faster. The economy loses gains from specialisation, and more of its work goes into hauling goods.

The settled economy at each transport cost

The horizontal axis is the transport cost on a log scale, as a multiple of the game's normal cost (1). The dot on the far right of each panel is autarky. Output per head is net output valued at one set of prices for every run (those at normal costs), so the runs compare like for like. It is shown with transport counted as an input to trade (solid) and as output in its own right (dashed).

Two of the panels have a hump. The share of workers in transport first rises with its cost, since each shipment takes more hauling, then falls when trade itself shrinks. The price gap between a good's dearest and cheapest tile widens as transport gets dearer, as you'd expect, but it narrows again near autarky. That is a quirk of the measure: it only covers goods still sold in two tiles or more, and at prohibitive costs the only such goods are those every tile makes for itself, so their prices are close.

Trade also moves the price of labour. Heckscher and Ohlin's theory, as Samuelson sharpened it in 1948, says that trade in goods alone tends to equalise what factors of production earn: a tile with plenty of labour exports goods that take a lot of labour, which raises its wages, and imports the rest. Wages can converge even if no one moves. To test it, the map was run at each transport cost twice, once with people free to move and once with nobody moving, and the spread of wages between tiles measured.

Trade in goods evens out wages

Cheap carriage also lets each tile do what it is relatively best at, which is Ricardo's comparative advantage. A plains tile need not grow the best wheat in the world to farm; it only needs farming to be what it does best compared with its neighbours. When carriage is dear, every tile must feed itself, so each grows grain and potatoes on whatever land it has. When carriage is cheap, the farming moves to the good farmland and the rest of the map buys its food. The right-hand chart shows each tile's share of workers on farms against how good its land is for farming, at nearly free carriage and at prohibitive carriage. The left-hand chart sums it up with Krugman's index of specialisation: how different each tile's mix of trades is from the map's as a whole.

Cheap carriage, specialised tiles

The transport cost need not be the same everywhere. A canal, a turnpike or a railway makes carriage cheap along one line, and towns grew up along them: the Erie Canal made New York the largest city in America, and the railways drew people into the towns on their lines and out of those they passed by. To test it, the coast's cheap shipping was taken away so that every hop costs the same, and then a route was laid diagonally across the map, from the top-left corner to the bottom-right, on which a hop costs a tenth of the usual. shows the trade and the people before and after.

Trade leaves the coasts for the route, and the people follow it. The tiles on the route can sell to the whole length of it and buy from it for little, so their firms grow, their wages rise and people move in until living there is no better than living off it. The route's tiles fill up and the tiles away from it empty. This is the spatial equilibrium of urban economics (Rosen 1979, Roback 1982): where people can move, an advantage of place shows up in how many live there more than in how well they live.

IV.People choose, and move to where life is better

The people

want
the best living they can afford: the most utility for what they spend, utility being the sum over their needs of weight × log(1 + what they have of it ÷ what they need of it)
know
their tile's prices and their own cash, and how well people live in the neighbouring tiles
decide
how to spend a twentieth of their cash each day (the rest is savings that carry them through a bad month), across all their needs and the goods that meet each; and whether to move: a few move each day towards a neighbour where people live better, more when the gap is wide and the journey cheap
work
every worker works, at the going wage, for whichever firms hire. The wage rises when firms want more hands than there are, and falls when there are more hands than work

Each tile's people work for its firms, own them, and spend their income on needs in three tiers: basic needs (food, clothing and heat), then comforts, then luxuries. How well they meet those needs is their living standard, the game's utility per person. Within a need, goods substitute for each other: bread and potatoes both meet the need for staples, meat and fish for protein, wood and coal for heat. People spend each day to get the most out of their income at today's prices. (In the game populations also grow when people eat well; on this page that is switched off, so that the experiments compare like with like.)

So when one good gets dear, people buy its substitute. To see it, a blight was let loose on the map's potatoes: every potato farm makes half what it did, as in the potato blight of the 1840s. compares it with a run without the blight.

A potato blight: people turn to bread

What people buy also changes with what they earn. In 1857 Ernst Engel found that the poorer a Belgian family, the larger the share of its budget went on food. Mercury's people behave the same way: a basic need is satisfied quickly, so as income grows a smaller share of it goes on bread, clothes and fuel, and more on comforts and luxuries. compares the map's tiles.

Engel's law: the richer, the smaller the share spent on basics

People also move. Every day, along each link between neighbouring tiles, a small share of the worse-off tile's workers moves to the better-off one:

share moving a day = × gap ÷ (1 + cost of the hop)

The gap is how much better the destination lives, as a share of the origin's living standard, counted up to 30%. Cheap hops along the coast carry more people than hard ones overland, and a tile never empties below workers.

To watch migration at work, one inland tile's firms were made half again as productive, as if coal or gold had been struck there. A twin run without the strike is the control. compares the two.

A strike in : who moves, and how far

The strike lifts living standards in the tile at once, and people start coming within weeks. They come first from the next tiles, which lose people almost as fast as the struck tile gains them, and then from two hops out. Tiles further away barely notice. Migration is slow on purpose: a gap of a fifth in living standards moves several percent of a tile's people a year, roughly the pace of the great migrations of the century.

Four years after the strike: people gained or lost

Blue tiles gained people compared with the control run; red tiles lost them. The ring of losers around the strike shows that people move one hop at a time.

Migration is what evens out living standards between places, and it works through the markets. The newcomers compete for jobs, so the struck tile's wages stop rising as fast as its output. They also need food, clothing and heat, so local demand rises, and with it the prices of what they buy. Higher prices and the extra mouths eat into the strike's gains until the gap with the neighbours is no longer worth the move. runs the strike twice more to show it: once as above, and once with nobody allowed to move, so that only trade can spread the strike's gains.

How migration evens out living standards

A boom in one trade draws workers from the others. When a coalfield in the nineteenth century found richer seams or better ways to dig them, as in South Wales or the Ruhr, its mines bid for labour, wages rose, and the valley's farms and workshops had to pay more for hands while their goods still sold at prices set by trade with the rest of the country. Some of them shrank.

To test it, the tile with the most coal miners finds its coal easier to dig, by a dose from 2 to 10 times, and each dose is run twice: once with nobody moving after the map has settled, and once with people free to move. A boom in a real coalfield came with the means to carry its coal (the Tyne's colliers, the Ruhr's canals and railways), and a tile's carters couldn't haul ten times the coal without them, so the boom tile's coal also gets as much cheaper to carry to its neighbours. The coal trade counts both the miners and the carters hauling the coal away; the rest of the tile's carters, shopkeepers and the like are its other services. shows how the tile's trades respond to the size of the boom.

A coal boom in

The boom levels off beyond about four times. The tile is the map's biggest coal producer, not a small one selling at prices set elsewhere, so the more it digs the lower the price its coal fetches from its neighbours, and past a point the extra coal is worth little more than it costs to dig.

V.Money, and a tariff

Everything so far has been about real things: what is made, where, and by whom. Prices have been in pounds, but nothing has said how many pounds there are. In Mercury the money is fixed: people, firms and merchants hold a stock of pounds that only changes hands. So what happens if there are more of them?

The quantity theory of money says: nothing real. Hume put it in 1752. If every coin in Britain were doubled overnight, prices would double, and the same goods would be made and traded for twice the pounds. To test it, the money every person, firm and merchant holds was doubled a year after the map had settled, and in another run halved. follows the price level (the cost of the basket people bought the day before), the average wage and real output (net output valued at the day before's prices), each against a run where nothing happened.

Money is neutral, but not at once

Halving the money costs output for a while, and doubling it doesn't, because a firm's cash is its working capital. When the money is halved, firms can't pay for the workers and materials they planned on until prices and wages have come down to match. Output falls until they have. Contemporaries saw the same after the Napoleonic wars, when Britain went back to gold at the old parity and prices fell for years. When the money is doubled nobody is short of cash, and prices simply rise.

The right-hand chart is Hume's own experiment, his price-specie flow. Double the money in one country alone and its prices rise above its neighbours'. It buys from them and sells to them less, and money flows out until prices are even again. In the end the extra money is spread over the whole map, and every price is a little higher.

Money flows across the map; so does grain, unless something stops it. From 1815 Britain's Corn Laws kept out foreign grain unless the price at home was high, until their repeal in 1846. Ricardo argued that they raised the price of bread, and with it the rents of those who owned the land that grew grain, at the expense of the workers who ate the bread and of the manufacturers who paid the workers. To test it, the map's two northern rows play Britain: hills and mountains with coal, iron and sheep, and a few plains. The rest of the map is the Continent. A duty is laid on grain entering Britain, at rates from none to an embargo, and what it raises goes back to Britain's people. shows Britain five years on.

The Corn Laws: dear bread, richer farms

Every effect points the way Ricardo said. The effects are small because Britain grows most of its own grain: about five sixths of it on the map, close to the share in the 1840s. Historians' estimates of what repeal did to Britain's economy as a whole are modest too. Where the effects are large is in who gets what. The duty moves income from those who eat bread to those who grow grain.

A duty on one good is a small barrier. In 2016 Britain voted to leave the European Union, and a "hard Brexit", leaving with no deal, would have put the EU's standard tariffs on Britain's trade with its largest market, and Britain's on the EU's: about 11% on farm goods and 4% on the rest. Most estimates of the cost, though, put the tariffs as the smaller part. Customs declarations, checks and proving where a good was made cost traders the equivalent of perhaps 5% to 15% of the value of what they ship. The Treasury's estimate before the vote was that leaving on such terms would leave Britain's output 7.5% lower after fifteen years. To test it, the same Britain gets three exits: the standard tariffs both ways; the tariffs and the border's paperwork, put at 9% of a good's value and paid in work, as extra carriage; and, for scale, an embargo that stops all trade. follows each against a run where nothing changed.

A hard Brexit: tariffs, paperwork, and an embargo

The tariffs and the paperwork cut trade across the border by a fifth and by two fifths, close to what has been measured since Britain left. Yet they cost Britain's people almost nothing, while the embargo costs them much more. The tariffs alone even leave Britain a fraction better off: its own duties push down what the Continent gets for the goods it sells to Britain, and the duties go back to Britain's people. This is the terms-of-trade argument for a tariff, which only works if the other side doesn't retaliate, and here it doesn't, since it charges only its standard rate. The trade a barrier stops is the trade that was only just worth doing: goods whose price gap barely covered the carriage. What Britain can't make at all, its sugar and its silk for fine clothes, keeps coming, because the gap in its price is far larger than any duty. Only the embargo stops that too, and Britain has to make those goods itself, worse and dearer. Mercury's trade is all of this kind. It has no services, no new varieties of goods, no firms growing more productive in bigger markets and no foreign investment, and those are the channels through which the estimates find most of Brexit's cost. So Mercury shows the part of the cost that comes from carrying goods, and that part is small.

VI.Under the bonnet

Mercury is written in Go and has no dependencies. A day on the classic map, with its 36 tiles, 1,068 firms, 108 groups of people and 5,950 trade routes, takes about two and a half milliseconds, some 400 days a second. A century, 36,500 days, runs in about a minute and a half. The world map has 2,105 tiles, 60,504 firms and 388,150 routes; there a day takes 66 milliseconds on four cores, 15 days a second, and 228 milliseconds on one.

What runs in parallel. Most of a day is each tile's own business: its firms and owners planning, its merchants planning their routes, its labour market and goods markets clearing, its merchants shipping, its firms producing and paying their owners, its people growing in number. That work runs on every core at once, a handful of tiles at a time. Four cores do the world map's day 3.4 times as fast as one. Maps of fewer than 64 tiles, the classic one among them, run on one core, as sharing them out would cost more than it saved.

What runs in order. Whatever crosses from one tile to another. A merchant who sells in a neighbour's market is owed the money there; people move along the links between tiles. While the tiles work in parallel, each writes only to its own state, and what it owes or is owed elsewhere goes into ledgers. Those are then settled one by one, tile by tile and route by route, in a fixed order.

Deterministic. So a day's result doesn't depend on how the work was shared out: forty days of the world map come out the same to the last bit on one core, three or four. Nothing is drawn at random, except owners' resolve to start a project, and that is a hash of the seed, the tile, the firm and how many projects it has started, not of the day. A change elsewhere moves only the decisions it really changes, so two versions of the economy can be compared run against run. The same world stepped the same number of days always gives the same state.

Saved and restored. The whole state of the economy can be written out and read back: every tile, firm, market and route, about a megabyte as text for the classic map. A world restored from it goes on exactly as the original would have. The experiments on this page use that to settle the map once and give every run its own copy of the settled map.
