Mercury

A deterministic, spatial, agent-based economy, where transport is a good like any other: produced by workers and bought by merchants.

Mercury runs an economy on a set of connected regions: a graph, whose regions are its nodes and whose connections are the routes between them. Each region has its own people, its own firms, and its own markets for labour and goods. Merchants carry goods along the connections and pay for the transport it takes. Firms hire workers and buy inputs, and owners build more capacity where it pays. People spend their income on what they need most, and move to where they live better.

Most models of trade treat transport as an "iceberg", where a fixed share of each shipment melts away on the way, or as a cost set from outside the model. In Mercury, carriage is made by carters, horses and boats, a trade like any other that hires its workers in each tile's labour market. Merchants buy it at the market price, in proportion to how far they ship and how bulky the good is. So the cost of trade is an outcome, not a parameter. It rises with wages and with the demand for carriage, and falls with better ways of carrying. When trade booms, carters compete with every other trade for workers.

Nothing in this economy is solved for. Four kinds of agent each want something simple, and act on it every day at the prices in front of them. The prices, the wages, the trade between tiles, what each tile specialises in and where people live all come out of what they do.

The agents meet in markets: every tile has one for labour and one for each good. The markets collect everyone's orders, and move prices a little each day towards where supply meets demand. Prices are in pounds, from a fixed stock of money that only changes hands.

The engine operates on a set of goods, needs, production recipes and a set of regions and their connections. Together these constitute an economy, and they're all defined in data files. Most experiments in this page use a simple example economy that resembles Europe ca. 1820, but the engine is not tied to this economy as the last example shows.

I.Firms make things

Every tile has a set of firms: farms, mines and forests that only work where the land allows, mills, smithies, tailors and breweries that can work anywhere, and its houses, which let shelter to its people. A firm hires labour from the tile's people, buys its inputs in the tile's market, and sells what it makes there. Its output follows a Cobb–Douglas production function:

output = TFP × levels1−s × laboura × input₁b × input₂c …, with a + b + c + … = s < 1

TFP is the recipe's productivity; levels are the firm's buildings, its capital; and each exponent is how much its factor counts.

The firm

How much does a firm make? Each day it looks at the prices in its tile, today's price for its good, the wage, and the prices of its inputs, and plans to make what pays best at those prices. It hires workers and buys inputs until the last worker or the last ton of coal adds exactly as much to its sales as it costs. Its target is the textbook competitive one: it makes up to the point where the price equals its marginal cost. It does this because it takes the price as given. A tile's "brewery" is not one firm but the tile's whole brewing industry, many small brewers none of whom can move the price of beer by brewing more or less, so it acts as a competitive industry does.

Owners add a level when the firm earns more than a level costs, and the exponents add up to less than one. So each extra worker in a given building adds less than the one before. shows this diminishing return for a single level, holding the other inputs at one unit each.

A firm can make its good in several ways, called methods. The first is the one available in 1820; later ones start switched off, as inventions not yet made, until whoever runs the economy switches them on. On this page every firm keeps its 1820 method; the later ones are shown only to compare them. A firm's level is its building and the machines in it. A later method's levels hold steam engines, tools or vehicles, which owners buy when they build or refit a level and replace as they wear out; it often needs fuel or materials the old way didn't, and makes more with the same hands.

Each extra worker adds less than the last

Land matters too. A farm on rich plains is half again as productive as the recipe says, and one in the tropics only six tenths. Mines need hills and mountains, and fishing needs the coast. gives these site multipliers for the land-bound firms that drive the map's trade.

Land is also a factor of production in its own right, fixed in each tile, so a farm that builds more on the same land gets less from each level, and land's share of what it makes is its rent. A tile's surface is shared by its farms, forests and houses, each holding land in proportion to the rent it pays, so a growing town builds over its fields within a few years. Mines, clay pits, wells and fisheries work deposits of their own.

II.People: workers and owners

The workers

want
the best living they can afford: the most utility for what they spend, utility being the sum over their needs of weight × log(1 + what they have of it ÷ what they need of it)
know
their tile's prices and their own cash, and how well people live in the neighbouring tiles
decide
how to spend a twentieth of their cash each day (the rest is savings that carry them through a bad month), across all their needs and the goods that meet each; and whether to move: a few move each day towards a neighbour where people live better, more when the gap is wide and the journey cheap
work
at the going wage, for whichever firms hire. The wage rises when firms want more hands than there are, and falls when there are more hands than work

The owners

want
a return on their capital: a new level must repay its cost within ten years of profit, about 10% a year, what nineteenth-century firms asked of new works
know
what each of their tile's firms earns, and what a level of each of its methods would earn at today's prices
decide
to build a level where it would pay, to refit one to a better method where the difference pays, or to let a level that keeps losing money go. Each owner acts in its own time, so the clear winners are taken up within months and the marginal ones over years
and
they take the firms' and merchants' cash beyond what those need as dividends, put cash in when a firm runs short, and spend what they receive like everyone else (one person in twenty-five is an owner)

Each tile's people are of two kinds: workers, who work for its firms at the going wage, and owners, who own the firms and live off their profits. Both spend their income on needs in three tiers: basic needs (food, clothing, heat and shelter), then comforts, then luxuries. How well they meet those needs is their living standard, their utility per person. Within a need, goods substitute for each other: bread and potatoes both meet the need for staples, meat and fish for protein, wood and coal for heat; shelter is a tile's own houses, let at a rent. People spend each day to get the most out of their income at today's prices. (People also multiply when they eat well; on this page that is switched off, so that the experiments compare like with like.)

So when one good gets dear, people buy its substitute. To see it, a blight was let loose on the map's potatoes: every potato farm makes half what it did, as in the potato blight of the 1840s. compares it with a run without the blight.

A potato blight: people turn to bread

What people buy also changes with what they earn. In 1857 Ernst Engel found that the poorer a Belgian family, the larger the share of its budget went on food. Mercury's people behave the same way: a basic need is satisfied quickly, so as income grows a smaller share of it goes on bread, clothes, fuel and rent, and more on comforts and luxuries. compares the map's tiles.

Engel's law: the richer, the smaller the share spent on basics

III.Markets set prices; merchants move goods

The market

is
a place where the agents trade: one for every good in every tile, and one for labour
each day
takes everyone's orders at today's price. If buyers want more than sellers offer, each buyer gets the same share of what was offered, and the other way round. Then it moves the price by the excess: up when buyers wanted more, down when sellers did, by a tenth of the gap a day at most

Between neighbouring tiles run merchants. A merchant buys a good where it is cheap and ships it to a neighbour where it is dear, if the price gap pays for the journey. Shipping uses up transport, a good of its own: carters, horses, boats and later railways, made by transport firms from labour. Each unit carried needs transport in proportion to the distance and the good's bulk, and perishables lose part of themselves on the way. Merchants trade only with neighbours; a good crosses the map by being sold on from tile to tile.

The merchants

are

want
to buy cheap and sell dear: a route earns the price where it sells, on what arrives, less the price where it buys and the transport it needs
know
the prices at both ends of each route, and what is still unsold at the far end
decide
how much to buy on each route: a little more each day while the margin is positive, a little less when it isn't, and less again when goods are left unsold at the far end. When the purse is short, the routes with the best margins are funded first

Merchants carry a good to where it is dear

Colour is the price, averaged over the last 30 days of a run at normal transport costs. Arrows are the merchants' shipments, thicker for more. Goods flow downhill in price, from where they are made to where they are dear.

The merchants' trade pins prices to each other. Call a good's landed cost on a route the price it is bought for at the origin plus the transport a unit needs, allowing for what spoils on the way. If the destination pays more than that, the route makes money and the merchants ship more. Extra supply then pushes the destination's price down, and extra buying pushes the origin's up, until the gap is gone. If the destination pays less, nobody ships. So once markets settle, a good's price in a tile that imports it equals its neighbour's price plus the cost of carriage, and no tile's price can stay above that for long.

Prices settle at the neighbour's price plus carriage

Carriage is paid per unit carried, in proportion to the distance and the good's bulk, not to its value. Carrying a chest of tea costs as much as a chest of potatoes, but the tea is worth far more, so the same carriage is a small share of its price. Alchian and Allen pointed out the consequence in 1964: per-unit transport costs make goods worth much for their bulk travel further than cheap, bulky ones. Hummels and Skiba confirmed it in trade data forty years later, under the title "Shipping the good apples out".

tests it, with spoilage switched off so that only carriage decides how far a good goes. IV.What transport is worth

The cost of transport has one dial: a multiplier on the transport every hop takes. To see what it does, the same map was run for fifteen years at each of 25 costs, and the last year measured: from nearly free (a twentieth of the normal cost) to 128 times normal, and finally to autarky, where no route ever pays and every tile lives on what it makes itself.

As transport gets dearer every measure of well-being falls smoothly (). Living standards drop because each tile must make more of its own goods, including the ones its land is bad at, and people pay more for imports. Output per head falls even faster. The economy loses gains from specialisation, and more of its work goes into hauling goods.

The dearer the transport, the poorer the map

The horizontal axis is the transport cost on a log scale, as a multiple of the normal cost (1). The dot on the far right of each panel is autarky. Output per head is net output valued at one set of prices for every run (those at normal costs), so the runs compare like for like. It is shown with transport counted as an input to trade (solid) and as output in its own right (dashed).

Two of the panels have a hump. The share of workers in transport first rises with its cost, since each shipment takes more hauling, then falls when trade itself shrinks. The price gap between a good's dearest and cheapest tile widens as transport gets dearer, as you'd expect, but it narrows again near autarky. That is a quirk of the measure: it only covers goods still sold in two tiles or more, and at prohibitive costs the only such goods are those every tile makes for itself, so their prices are close.

Trade also moves the price of labour. Heckscher and Ohlin's theory, as Samuelson sharpened it in 1948, says that trade in goods alone tends to equalise what factors of production earn: a tile with plenty of labour exports goods that take a lot of labour, which raises its wages, and imports the rest. Wages can converge even if no one moves. To test it, the map was run at each transport cost twice, once with people free to move and once with nobody moving, and the spread of wages between tiles measured.

Trade in goods evens out wages

Cheap carriage also lets each tile do what it is relatively best at, which is Ricardo's comparative advantage. A plains tile need not grow the best wheat in the world to farm; it only needs farming to be what it does best compared with its neighbours. When carriage is dear, every tile must feed itself, so each grows grain and potatoes on whatever land it has. When carriage is cheap, the farming moves to the good farmland and the rest of the map buys its food. The right-hand chart shows each tile's share of workers on farms against how good its land is for farming, at nearly free carriage and at prohibitive carriage. The left-hand chart sums it up with Krugman's index of specialisation: how different each tile's mix of trades is from the map's as a whole.

Cheap carriage, specialised tiles

The transport cost need not be the same everywhere. A canal, a turnpike or a railway makes carriage cheap along one line, and towns grew up along them: the Erie Canal made New York the largest city in America, and the railways drew people into the towns on their lines and out of those they passed by. To test it, the coast's cheap shipping was taken away so that every hop costs the same, and then a route was laid diagonally across the map, from the top-left corner to the bottom-right, on which a hop costs a tenth of the usual. shows the trade before and after.

Trade leaves the coasts for the route. The tiles on the route can sell to the whole length of it and buy from it for little, so their firms grow and their wages rise. What that does to where people live comes in section V.

V.People move to where life is better

People also move. Every day, along each link between neighbouring tiles, a small share of the worse-off tile's workers moves to the better-off one:

share moving a day = × gap ÷ (1 + cost of the hop)

The gap is how much better the destination lives, as a share of the origin's living standard, counted up to 30%. Cheap hops along the coast carry more people than hard ones overland, and a tile never empties below workers.

To watch migration at work, one inland tile's firms were made half again as productive, as if coal or gold had been found there. A twin run without the windfall is the control. compares the two.

A windfall in draws people from next door

Left: people gained or lost compared with the control run, summed by distance from the windfall. Right: the living standard in the windfall tile, in the control run, and in its neighbours. The dashed line marks the windfall.

The windfall lifts living standards in the tile at once, and people start coming within weeks. They come first from the next tiles, which lose people almost as fast as the windfall tile gains them, and then from two hops out. Tiles further away barely notice. Migration is slow on purpose: a gap of a fifth in living standards moves several percent of a tile's people a year, roughly the pace of the great migrations of the century.

People move one hop at a time

Blue tiles gained people compared with the control run; red tiles lost them. The ring of losers around the windfall shows that people move one hop at a time.

Migration is what evens out living standards between places, and it works through the markets. The newcomers compete for jobs, so the windfall tile's wages stop rising as fast as its output. They also need food, clothing, heat and houses, so local demand rises, and with it the prices of what they buy. Higher prices and the extra mouths eat into the windfall's gains until the gap with the neighbours is no longer worth the move. runs the windfall twice more to show it: once as above, and once with nobody allowed to move, so that only trade can spread the windfall's gains.

The route's tiles fill up and the tiles away from it empty: their firms grow and their wages rise, and people move in until living there is no better than living off it. This is the spatial equilibrium of urban economics (Rosen 1979, Roback 1982): where people can move, an advantage of place shows up in how many live there more than in how well they live.

A boom in one trade draws workers from the others. When a coalfield in the nineteenth century found richer seams or better ways to dig them, as in South Wales or the Ruhr, its mines bid for labour, wages rose, and the valley's farms and workshops had to pay more for hands while their goods still sold at prices set by trade with the rest of the country. Some of them shrank.

To test it, the tile with the most coal miners finds its coal easier to dig, by a dose from 2 to 10 times, and each dose is run twice: once with nobody moving after the map has settled, and once with people free to move. shows how the tile's trades respond to the size of the boom.

A boom in a real coalfield came with the means to carry its coal away: the Tyne's colliers, the Ruhr's canals and railways. A tile's carters couldn't haul ten times the coal without them, so the boom tile's coal also gets as much cheaper to carry to its neighbours. The coal trade counts both the miners and the carters hauling the coal away; the rest of the tile's carters, shopkeepers and the like are its other services.

A coal boom in squeezes its other trades

The boom levels off beyond about four times. The tile is the map's biggest coal producer, not a small one selling at prices set elsewhere, so the more it digs the lower the price its coal fetches from its neighbours, and past a point the extra coal is worth little more than it costs to dig.

VI.Money and prices

In Mercury the stock of money is fixed. The pounds people, firms and merchants hold at the start are all there will ever be: they change hands, and none are made or destroyed. Every price on this page is in those pounds. What sets how many of them things cost? Economists tie money to prices with the quantity equation:

M · V = P · Y

M is the stock of money; V its velocity, how many times each pound is spent in a period; P the price level; and Y real output, the goods those pounds buy. As written it is true by definition: the pounds spent equal the goods bought times their prices. The quantity theory adds that V and Y are set by real things, by habits of spending and by what land, labour and capital can make, so M sets P.

In Mercury the equation is a rule of behaviour. Each day people spend a share s of the cash they hold, a twentieth in this economy, so

P · Y = s · M

where M is the money people hold, s its velocity, Y the goods they buy a day and P their prices. The money is fixed, and the land, labour and recipes set Y, so M and s set P. Firms and merchants hold money too, so M is the people's part of it, and that part can shift. Four experiments test the equation: doubling every price and wage, which breaks it; doubling or halving the money, M; doubling how fast it is spent, s; and doubling what the economy can make, Y.

The first doubles every price and every wage on the map on one day, the money left as it was, against a twin where nothing happens. If the equation holds, the same pounds buy half as much, and the markets bring prices back down. follows it.

Double every price and wage, and the economy undoes it

The second experiment changes M. Hume put the quantity theory's prediction in 1752: if every coin in Britain were doubled overnight, prices would double, and the same goods would be made and traded for twice the pounds. To test it, the money every person, firm and merchant holds was doubled once the map had settled, and in another run halved. follows the price level (the cost of the basket people bought the day before), the average wage and real output (net output valued at the day before's prices), each against a run where nothing happened.

Money is neutral, but not at once

The third doubles s: from one day everyone spends a tenth of their cash a day instead of a twentieth, against a twin where nothing changes. If the equation holds, prices double and nothing real changes. follows it, with the share of the map's money that people hold.

VII.Trade policy

This section puts barriers on trade and sees what they do: a duty on grain, a border with Europe, and a monopoly that only imports can check.

From 1815 Britain's Corn Laws kept out foreign grain unless the price at home was high, until their repeal in 1846. Ricardo argued that they raised the price of bread, and with it the rents of those who owned the land that grew grain, at the expense of the workers who ate the bread and of the manufacturers who paid the workers. To test it, the map's two northern rows play Britain: hills and mountains with coal, iron and sheep, and a few plains. The rest of the map is the Continent. A duty is laid on grain entering Britain, at rates from none to an embargo, and what it raises goes back to Britain's people. shows Britain five years on.

The Corn Laws: dear bread, richer farms

A duty on one good is a small barrier. In 2016 Britain voted to leave the European Union, and a "hard Brexit", leaving with no deal, would have put the EU's standard tariffs on Britain's trade with its largest market, and Britain's on the EU's: about 11% on farm goods and 4% on the rest. Most estimates of the cost, though, put the tariffs as the smaller part. Customs declarations, checks and proving where a good was made cost traders the equivalent of perhaps 5% to 15% of the value of what they ship. The Treasury's estimate before the vote was that leaving on such terms would leave Britain's output 7.5% lower after fifteen years. To test it, the same Britain gets three exits: the standard tariffs both ways; the tariffs and the border's paperwork, put at 9% of a good's value and paid in work, as extra carriage; and, for scale, an embargo that stops all trade. follows each against a run where nothing changed.

A hard Brexit costs little; an embargo costs much

The tariffs alone cost Britain almost nothing for a second reason too: its own duties push down what the Continent gets for the goods it sells to Britain, and the duties go back to Britain's people. This terms-of-trade gain, the old argument for a tariff, offsets most of what the tariffs cost. And the trade a barrier stops is the trade that was only just worth doing: goods whose price gap barely covered the carriage. What Britain can't make at all, its sugar and its silk for fine clothes, keeps coming, because the gap in its price is far larger than any duty. Only the embargo stops that too, and Britain has to make those goods itself, worse and dearer.

Mercury's trade is all of this kind. It has no services, no new varieties of goods, no firms growing more productive in bigger markets and no foreign investment, and those are the channels through which the estimates find most of Brexit's cost. So Mercury shows the part of the cost that comes from carrying goods, and that part is small.

Every firm on this page so far has been a whole industry acting as a competitive one does, taking prices as given. This last experiment changes that, to see how trade and monopoly interact: the model is not meant to have monopolies, and none of the other experiments has one.

A monopoly plans differently. As the only maker of its good, it knows that selling more lowers the price, so a unit more earns it only its marginal revenue: the price, less what the lower price loses on all the rest. It makes up to the point where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. That is less than a competitive industry would make, and it charges more. With demand of elasticity ε, the monopoly's last unit earns the price times (1 − 1/ε), so it sets a markup over marginal cost. Here, for once, the model is changed: one tile's brewery becomes a monopoly, facing demand of elasticity 2 (beer substitutes for tea at that elasticity). Its rivals are the merchants who bring beer from the neighbouring tiles, as far as carriage allows: the more of the tile's beer they bring, the smaller the monopoly's share, and the less its own sales move the price. So that only carriage stands in their way, beer here doesn't spoil (in the example economy a tenth of it is lost on every hop). repeats this at each cost of carrying beer, from four times the normal to nearly free, as if beer were that much bulkier or lighter; everything else carries as usual, so each run is the same economy but for beer.

Free trade erodes a monopoly

VIII.Another economy: star lanes

Every experiment so far has run the example economy of Europe around 1820. This one runs a different economy on the same engine, with its own data and its own map, a graph of star systems joined by lanes.

Take an empire of twelve star systems, joined by a sparse web of lanes of different lengths. People eat rations and fruit, drink water, wear textiles and keep nanobots to run their homes. Food and water have a subsistence level: below it nothing else a person has is worth much, and people starve.

Every world can feed itself the simple way, by automated vertical farming and integrated water reclamation, but that feeds only a few billion on each, about as many as such methods could feed on Earth: some 90 billion across the empire. Its 650 billion live on the productive way: direct nutrition printing on the garden worlds and off-world water harvesting from the ice and ocean worlds, run on water and nanobots shipped in from other systems. So the empire lives by trade.

Its trade runs on one thing. Every ship between systems burns aether, a fuel found on one barren world, Cinder. Freight, the transport merchants buy, is made from labour and aether, so a system with no aether can receive goods but can't send any.

The engine's units of time and of people mean whatever the economy on top says. Here a tick is a day, from 2300, and each of the economy's people stands for a billion.

IX.Under the bonnet

Mercury is written in Go and has no dependencies. A day on the classic map, with its 36 tiles, 1,068 firms, 72 groups of people (a group of workers and one of owners in each tile) and 5,950 trade routes, takes about three milliseconds, some 350 days a second. A century, 36,500 days, runs in under two minutes. The world map has 2,105 tiles, 60,504 firms and 388,150 routes; there a day takes 66 milliseconds on four cores, 15 days a second, and 228 milliseconds on one.

What runs in parallel. Most of a day is each tile's own business: its firms and owners planning, its merchants planning their routes, its labour market and goods markets clearing, its merchants shipping, its firms producing and paying their owners, its people growing in number. That work runs on every core at once, a handful of tiles at a time. Four cores do the world map's day 3.4 times as fast as one. Maps of fewer than 64 tiles, the classic one among them, run on one core, as sharing them out would cost more than it saved.

What runs in order. Whatever crosses from one tile to another. A merchant who sells in a neighbour's market is owed the money there; people move along the links between tiles. While the tiles work in parallel, each writes only to its own state, and what it owes or is owed elsewhere goes into ledgers. Those are then settled one by one, tile by tile and route by route, in a fixed order.

Deterministic. So a day's result doesn't depend on how the work was shared out: forty days of the world map come out the same to the last bit on one core, three or four. Nothing is drawn at random, so a change moves only the decisions it really changes, and two versions of the economy can be compared run against run. The same world stepped the same number of days always gives the same state.

Saved and restored. The whole state of the economy can be written out and read back: every tile, firm, market and route, about a megabyte as text for the classic map. A world restored from it goes on exactly as the original would have. The experiments on this page use that to settle the map once and give every run its own copy of the settled map.
