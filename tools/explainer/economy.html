The Mercury Economy

How thirty-six tiles of farms, mills and merchants feed, clothe and move their people, with no state to steer them, and what happens when moving goods gets cheaper.

Mercury is a game about the nineteenth century, but underneath the nations, armies and treaties sits an economy that runs on its own. This page takes the nations away and looks at that economy alone: no taxes, no tariffs, no roads built by a state, no new inventions. What is left is land, people, firms, markets and merchants, on the game's classic map of thirty-six hexagonal tiles.

Every number and chart below comes from running that map in the game's own engine. Click a tile on the map to see what its people do for a living once the map has settled.

Plate I

The world: terrain, and what each tile does

Each hexagon is a tile, with its own people, firms and market. Tiles on the edge or next to the sea are coastal: they can fish, and ships carry goods past them cheaply.

I.Firms make things

Every tile has a set of firms: farms, mines and forests that only work where the land allows, and mills, smithies, tailors and breweries that can work anywhere. A firm hires labour from the tile's people, buys its inputs in the tile's market, and sells what it makes there. Its output follows a Cobb–Douglas production function:

output = TFP × levels1−s × laboura × input₁b × input₂c …, with a + b + c + … = s < 1

Levels are the firm's buildings, its capital. Owners add a level when the firm earns more than a level costs, and the exponents add up to less than one. So each extra worker in a given building adds less than the one before. Plate II shows this diminishing return for a single level, holding the other inputs at one unit each.

A firm can make its good in several ways, called methods. The first is the one available in 1820; later ones come with technology. In the full game nations research those technologies; here the methods are shown only to compare them. A later method is more productive (higher TFP) and often needs fewer hands (a lower labour exponent), but needs machinery or fuel the old way didn't.

Land matters too. A farm on rich plains is half again as productive as the recipe says, and one in the tropics only six tenths. Mines need hills and mountains, and fishing needs the coast. Plate III gives these site multipliers for the land-bound firms that drive the map's trade.

Each bar is the multiplier on a firm's productivity on that terrain (1 = the recipe as written). A missing bar means the firm can't work there at all.

Put together, land, people and prices decide what each tile does and how well work pays there. Plate IV maps the value a worker adds in a day (what the tile's firms sell, less what they buy, per worker).

II.Markets set prices; merchants move goods

Each tile has a market for every good. The price rises when buyers want more than sellers offer and falls when there is too much. Nobody sets prices; they move a little each day toward the level where supply and demand meet.

Between neighbouring tiles run merchants. A merchant buys a good where it is cheap and ships it to a neighbour where it is dear, if the price gap pays for the journey. Shipping uses up transport, a good of its own: carters, horses, boats and later railways, made by transport firms from labour. Each unit carried needs transport in proportion to the distance and the good's bulk, and perishables lose part of themselves on the way. Merchants trade only with neighbours; a good crosses the map by being sold on from tile to tile.

Plate V

The price of a good, and where it flows

Colour is the price, averaged over the last 30 days of a run at normal transport costs. Arrows are the merchants' shipments, thicker for more. Goods flow downhill in price, from where they are made to where they are dear.

The merchants' trade pins prices to each other. Call a good's landed cost on a route the price it is bought for at the origin plus the transport a unit needs, allowing for what spoils on the way. If the destination pays more than that, the route makes money and the merchants ship more. Extra supply then pushes the destination's price down, and extra buying pushes the origin's up, until the gap is gone. If the destination pays less, nobody ships. So once markets settle, a good's price in a tile that imports it equals its neighbour's price plus the cost of carriage, and no tile's price can stay above that for long.

Plate VI

Prices settle at the neighbour's price plus carriage

Carriage is paid per unit carried, in proportion to the distance and the good's bulk, not to its value. Carrying a chest of tea costs as much as a chest of potatoes, but the tea is worth far more, so the same carriage is a small share of its price. Alchian and Allen pointed out the consequence in 1964: per-unit transport costs make goods worth much for their bulk travel further than cheap, bulky ones. Hummels and Skiba confirmed it in trade data forty years later, under the title "Shipping the good apples out".

Plate VII tests it, with spoilage switched off so that only carriage decides how far a good goes. Each dot is a good: how many hops a unit travels on average, counting every time it is sold on, against its value per unit of bulk. Then the bulk of potatoes, coal and tea is changed, one good at a time, and the arrows show where each moves. Make potatoes four times less bulky, as if they were dried or the carts got bigger, and they move up and to the right: they travel five times as far.

III.What transport is worth

Transport cost is one number in the game that scales every hop. To see what it does, the same map was run for fifteen years at each of 25 costs, and the last year measured: from nearly free (a twentieth of the normal cost) to 128 times normal, and finally to autarky, where no route ever pays and every tile lives on what it makes itself.

As transport gets dearer every measure of well-being falls smoothly (Plate VIII). Living standards drop because each tile must make more of its own goods, including the ones its land is bad at, and people pay more for imports. Output per head falls even faster. The economy loses gains from specialisation, and more of its work goes into hauling goods.

Plate VIII

The settled economy at each transport cost

The horizontal axis is the transport cost on a log scale, as a multiple of the game's normal cost (1). The dot on the far right of each panel is autarky. Output per head is net output valued at one set of prices for every run (those at normal costs), so the runs compare like for like. It is shown with transport counted as an input to trade (solid) and as output in its own right (dashed).

Two of the panels have a hump. The share of workers in transport first rises with its cost, since each shipment takes more hauling, then falls when trade itself shrinks. The price gap between a good's dearest and cheapest tile widens as transport gets dearer, as you'd expect, but it narrows again near autarky. That is a quirk of the measure: it only covers goods still sold in two tiles or more, and at prohibitive costs the only such goods are those every tile makes for itself, so their prices are close.

IV.People choose, and move to where life is better

Each tile's people work for its firms, own them, and spend their income on needs in three tiers: basic needs (food, clothing and heat), then comforts, then luxuries. How well they meet those needs is their living standard, the game's utility per person. Within a need, goods substitute for each other: bread and potatoes both meet the need for staples, meat and fish for protein, wood and coal for heat. People spend each day to get the most out of their income at today's prices. (In the game populations also grow when people eat well; on this page that is switched off, so that the experiments compare like with like.)

So when one good gets dear, people buy its substitute. To see it, a blight was let loose on the map's potatoes: every potato farm makes half what it did, as in the potato blight of the 1840s. Plate IX compares it with a run without the blight.

Plate IX

A potato blight: people turn to bread

People also move. Every day, along each link between neighbouring tiles, a small share of the worse-off tile's workers moves to the better-off one:

share moving a day = × gap ÷ (1 + cost of the hop)

The gap is how much better the destination lives, as a share of the origin's living standard, counted up to 30%. Cheap hops along the coast carry more people than hard ones overland, and a tile never empties below workers.

To watch migration at work, one inland tile's firms were made half again as productive, as if coal or gold had been struck there. A twin run without the strike is the control. Plate X compares the two.

Plate X

A strike in : who moves, and how far

Left: people gained or lost compared with the control run, summed by distance from the strike. Right: the living standard in the struck tile, in the control run, and in its neighbours. The dashed line marks the strike.

The strike lifts living standards in the tile at once, and people start coming within weeks. They come first from the next tiles, which lose people almost as fast as the struck tile gains them, and then from two hops out. Tiles further away barely notice. Migration is slow on purpose: a gap of a fifth in living standards moves several percent of a tile's people a year, roughly the pace of the great migrations of the century.

Plate XI

Four years after the strike: people gained or lost

Brass tiles gained people compared with the control run; red tiles lost them. The ring of losers around the strike shows that people move one hop at a time.

Migration is what evens out living standards between places, and it works through the markets. The newcomers compete for jobs, so the struck tile's wages stop rising as fast as its output. They also need food, clothing and heat, so local demand rises, and with it the prices of what they buy. Higher prices and the extra mouths eat into the strike's gains until the gap with the neighbours is no longer worth the move. Plate XII runs the strike twice more to show it: once as above, and once with nobody allowed to move, so that only trade can spread the strike's gains.

How these numbers were made. The game's classic map (36 tiles) with no nations: no state, taxes, armies, roads or new technology, so firms keep their 1820 methods. Population growth is switched off; migration and investment are on. Each run first settles for fifteen years, as owners build up their firms and people sort themselves between tiles, and then runs its experiment for five years (the transport sweep measures the fifteenth year at each cost). All start from the same state. Without nations nothing in the economy is drawn at random, so a run is exactly repeatable. The transport sweep is the engine's TestTransportSweep; the tiles, the routes, the Alchian–Allen runs, the blight, the strike and the recipes come from TestExplainerData. tools/explainer/build.sh rebuilds this page from both.
